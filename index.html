Standard Operating Systems mathematical definitions and metrics for Round Robin (RR) scheduling

Timestamp

Completion Time (CT)

t = CT

The exact simulation clock timestamp at which a process finishes its final remaining CPU burst instruction and departs from the scheduling arena.

Duration

Turnaround Time (TAT)

TAT = CT − AT

The total lifespan elapsed from the exact moment the process enters the system (Arrival Time, AT) until its final instruction completes (Completion Time, CT).

Duration

Waiting Time (WT)

The total amount of time a process spends waiting in the Ready Queue for CPU execution. Derived by subtracting actual CPU processing time (Burst Time, BT) from total turnaround lifespan.

Aggregate Metric

Average Turnaround Time

The arithmetic mean turnaround time across all N scheduled processes. Evaluates overall system turnaround efficiency and batch latency.

Aggregate Metric

Average Waiting Time

Average WT = (Σ WT) / N

The arithmetic mean waiting time across all N scheduled processes. Reflects how quickly ready tasks gain access to CPU execution without prolonged starvation.

Efficiency Ratio

CPU Utilization

CPU Utilization = (Total Busy Time / Total Elapsed Time) × 100%

The percentage of total simulation time that the CPU was actively processing a process burst rather than standing idle awaiting new arrivals.

1. CPU Scheduling Foundations

Core OS Concept
▼

CPU Scheduling is the core operating system mechanism that decides which ready process in memory receives CPU execution time when the processor becomes idle.

In modern multiprogramming systems, multiple processes reside in memory simultaneously. By switching the CPU among processes, the operating system ensures maximum productivity and responsiveness.

🎯 Primary Objectives of CPU Scheduling
  • Max CPU Utilization: Keep the processor busy 100% of the time (typically 40%–90% in real workloads).
  • Max Throughput: Maximize the number of processes completed per unit of time.
  • Min Turnaround Time: Minimize elapsed time from process submission to full completion.
  • Min Waiting Time: Minimize time spent sitting idly in the Ready Queue.
  • Min Response Time: Provide swift initial response for interactive user programs.
The CPU–I/O Burst Cycle: Process execution alternates between CPU bursts (performing arithmetic and logic) and I/O bursts (waiting for disk, network, or keyboard). Scheduling takes place during transitions between these states!
⚔️ Preemptive vs. Non-Preemptive Scheduling
  • Non-Preemptive (Cooperative): Once a process takes the CPU, it keeps control until it terminates or voluntarily yields to wait for I/O (e.g., standard FCFS).
  • Preemptive: The operating system can forcibly interrupt an executing process and reallocate the CPU to another process via hardware timer interrupts (e.g., Round Robin).
🔄

2. Round Robin (RR) Scheduling Algorithm

Algorithm Mechanics
▼

Round Robin (RR) is one of the oldest, simplest, fairest, and most widely implemented scheduling algorithms, specially designed for time-sharing systems.

Rather than letting a CPU-intensive job monopolize the processor, Round Robin allocates a fixed, equal slice of CPU execution time—called a Time Quantum ($q$)—to each process in circular order.

🛠️ How the Algorithm Operates (Step-by-Step)
  1. FIFO Ready Queue: The scheduler maintains all runnable processes in a circular First-In, First-Out queue (just like the conveyor belt in our Arena!).
  2. Dispatching: The process at the head of the queue is dispatched to the CPU and assigned a timer set to the Time Quantum ($q$).
  3. Case A — Finishes Early ($\text{Remaining BT} \le q$): The process completes before or at quantum expiration. It exits to the Completed Lounge and immediately yields the CPU. No quantum time is wasted!
  4. Case B — Preempted ($\text{Remaining BT} > q$): When the timer expires, an OS hardware interrupt triggers. A context switch saves the process's registers, and the preempted process is appended to the tail of the Ready Queue.
  5. Tie-Breaking at Quantum Boundary: If a new process arrives at the exact instant another process's quantum expires, the newly arriving process is admitted to the queue first, and the preempted process is appended after it.
Starvation-Free Guarantee: If there are $n$ processes in the ready queue and the time quantum is $q$, each process receives $1/n$ of the CPU power in chunks of at most $q$ units. No process ever waits longer than $(n - 1) \times q$ time units for its next turn!
⏱️

3. Time Quantum (Time Slice) & Context Switch Overhead

Crucial Trade-off
▼

The Time Quantum ($q$) is the maximum continuous duration of CPU time allocated to any process during a single round. Its size determines the entire performance profile of the system.

⚖️ The Quantum Dilemma: Too Large vs. Too Small

⚠️ If Quantum is Too Large ($q \to \infty$)

Round Robin degrades into First-Come, First-Served (FCFS).

  • Interactive short processes get stuck waiting behind long batch jobs (the Convoy Effect).
  • Response time increases significantly.
  • No preemption occurs during normal execution.

⚠️ If Quantum is Too Small ($q \to 0$)

Approaches theoretical Processor Sharing, but crippled by overhead.

  • Frequent context switches consume substantial CPU cycles saving/restoring program counters and registers.
  • Cache thrashing occurs as CPU caches are repeatedly invalidated.
  • Effective throughput drops drastically.
Golden Rule of Thumb: In production operating systems (Linux, Windows), time quanta typically range from 10ms to 100ms, while context switches take $< 10\mu\text{s}$ (less than $0.1\%$ overhead). A practical rule is that 80% of CPU bursts should be shorter than the time quantum.

4. Arrival Time (AT) & Burst Time (BT)

Process Attributes
▼

Every process admitted into the scheduling arena is characterized by its timing properties:

Arrival Time (AT)
t = AT ≥ 0
The exact simulation timestamp at which the process arrives in the system and becomes ready for dispatch in the Ready Queue.
Burst Time (BT) / CPU Burst
BT > 0
The total amount of CPU runtime required by the process to execute completely and finish its computation.
Remaining Burst Time
Rem(t) = BT - Executed(t)
Decrements by 1 every time the process executes for one second in the CPU. When Rem reaches 0, the process enters COMPLETED status.
Completion Time (CT)
t = CT
The clock time at which the process finishes its final instruction and vacates the CPU chamber forever.

5. Formulas, Metrics & Step-by-Step Worked Example

Formulas & Math
▼

To evaluate and grade CPU scheduling efficiency, operating systems measure three critical metrics:

1. Turnaround Time (TAT)
TAT = CT − AT
Total elapsed lifespan of the process in the operating system from submission to completion.
2. Waiting Time (WT)
WT = TAT − BT
Total duration the process spent idling in the Ready Queue waiting for CPU turns.
3. Response Time (RT)
RT = FirstRun − AT
Time from submission to the very first moment the CPU allocates a slice to this process.
4. CPU Utilization
Util = (Busy / Total) × 100%
The percentage of total simulation time that the CPU was actively executing user processes.

Given: Three processes with Time Quantum $q = 2$:
• P1: $\text{AT}=0, \text{BT}=5$  |  • P2: $\text{AT}=1, \text{BT}=3$  |  • P3: $\text{AT}=2, \text{BT}=1$

Step-by-Step Gantt Execution Timeline:
P1[0 → 2]
P2[2 → 4]
P3[4 → 5] ⭐
P1[5 → 7]
P2[7 → 8] 🏁
P1[8 → 9] 🏁
  • t = 0 to 2: P1 runs for 2 units (rem: 3). P2 arrives at $t=1$, P3 arrives at $t=2$. Ready Queue: [P2, P3, P1].
  • t = 2 to 4: P2 runs for 2 units (rem: 1). Ready Queue: [P3, P1, P2].
  • t = 4 to 5: P3 runs for 1 unit (rem: 0). P3 completes early at $t=5$ (quantum not wasted!). $\text{CT}=5$. Ready Queue: [P1, P2].
  • t = 5 to 7: P1 runs for 2 units (rem: 1). Ready Queue: [P2, P1].
  • t = 7 to 8: P2 runs for 1 unit (rem: 0). P2 completes at $t=8$! $\text{CT}=8$. Ready Queue: [P1].
  • t = 8 to 9: P1 runs for its final 1 unit (rem: 0). P1 completes at $t=9$! $\text{CT}=9$. All done!

P1 0 5 9 9 − 0 = 9 9 − 5 = 4 0 − 0 = 0
P2 1 3 8 8 − 1 = 7 7 − 3 = 4 2 − 1 = 1
P3 2 1 5 5 − 2 = 3 3 − 1 = 2 4 − 2 = 2

• Average Waiting Time = (4 + 4 + 2) / 3 = 10 / 3 = 3.33 s
• CPU Utilization = (9 busy / 9 elapsed) × 100% = 100.0%

6. Advantages & Disadvantages of Round Robin

Evaluation
▼

Understanding trade-offs is essential for system designers and computer science exams:

✅ Advantages

  • Complete Starvation Freedom: Every process receives a regular CPU slice within bounded time $(n - 1) \times q$.
  • Fairness: CPU time is apportioned equitably without bias towards short or high-priority processes.
  • Superb Interactive Response: Ideal for desktop operating systems, interactive shells, and cloud web servers where quick initial feedback is paramount.
  • Simple Implementation: Relies only on a clean circular FIFO queue and a hardware timer interrupt.

❌ Disadvantages

  • Higher Average Waiting Time: Often yields higher average turnaround and waiting times compared to Shortest Job First (SJF).
  • Context Switch Overhead: If the time quantum is chosen too small, excessive context switching degrades overall throughput.
  • Tuning Sensitivity: Performance hinges entirely on picking an optimal Time Quantum suited to the workload.
  • Does Not Prioritize Urgent Tasks: Pure Round Robin treats critical background maintenance and urgent user interactions identically.

RR — Round Robin Scheduling

Preemptive Algorithm

Round Robin (RR) is a preemptive CPU scheduling algorithm in which each process gets the CPU for a fixed amount of time called the time quantum. The processes are maintained in a ready queue and are executed one after another in circular order. If a process finishes within its time quantum, it moves to the completed state; otherwise, it is preempted and placed at the end of the ready queue to wait for its next turn. Round Robin provides fair CPU access and good responsiveness, especially in time-sharing systems. However, a very small time quantum can cause excessive context switching, while a very large time quantum makes the algorithm behave more like First Come, First Served scheduling.
